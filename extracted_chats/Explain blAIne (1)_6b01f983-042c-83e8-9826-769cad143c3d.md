## 2. Problem Definition

Current AI systems often operate as isolated applications or model endpoints. A user may need to select models, transfer information between systems, evaluate conflicting responses, track sources, and determine whether an automated action is safe or authorized.

This creates several challenges:

- Specialized capabilities are difficult to coordinate consistently.
- Model outputs may conflict or contain unsupported information.
- Information can lose its provenance as it moves between systems.
- Model-generated recommendations may be confused with verified decisions.
- Automated execution can exceed the authority intended by the user.
- Sensitive information may be exposed through external services.
- Model adaptation and specialization can become disconnected from operational evaluation.

blAIne addresses these challenges through a governed orchestration architecture.

The research objective is to investigate how specialized AI models and systems can be coordinated within a locally controlled environment while maintaining traceability, evaluation, human authorization, and controlled execution.

## 3. Platform Definition and Scope

blAIne is the orchestration and governance platform responsible for coordinating participating AI models and systems.

Its intended responsibilities include:

- Receiving and interpreting user requests.
- Identifying the capabilities required to fulfill a request.
- Decomposing complex tasks into manageable subtasks.
- Selecting appropriate specialized models or systems.
- Delegating work and managing execution.
- Collecting and evaluating results.
- Identifying uncertainty, conflicts, and incomplete information.
- Synthesizing results while preserving relevant provenance.
- Determining when human review or authorization is required.
- Managing authorized operations through defined controls.
- Maintaining records sufficient for inspection and audit.

The platform is not intended to make human oversight an afterthought. Governance is a foundational part of its architecture.

- A single AI model.

- An unrestricted autonomous agent.
- A collection of unrelated software projects.

- A claim that every model output is inherently reliable.

## 6. Intended Execution Lifecycle

The following describes the target operational workflow.

1. **Request:** A human submits an objective or task.
2. **Interpretation:** blAIne identifies the intended outcome, constraints, and relevant context.
3. **Planning:** The orchestration layer decomposes the task into subtasks.
4. **Capability selection:** Appropriate models or participating systems are identified.
5. **Delegation:** Tasks are assigned within defined permissions.
6. **Execution:** Specialized systems perform their assigned work.
7. **Collection:** Results are returned with relevant source and execution information.
8. **Evaluation:** blAIne examines completeness, consistency, uncertainty, and evidence.
9. **Conflict handling:** Disagreements or unsupported results are identified and addressed.
10. **Synthesis:** Results are combined while retaining relevant provenance.
11. **Authorization:** Operations requiring human approval are presented for review.
12. **Execution:** Authorized operations are carried out through controlled mechanisms.
13. **Recording:** Relevant events and outcomes are recorded.
14. **Review:** Results and system behavior can be inspected and evaluated.

This lifecycle is an architectural target. Its individual stages must be implemented and tested before the complete workflow can be claimed as operational.

## 7. Governance Model

### 7.1 Human Authority

Human authority is the root of the platform's governance model.

Humans establish objectives, permissions, constraints, and approval requirements.

AI systems may assist with reasoning, planning, evaluation, and execution, but they do not independently acquire authority beyond the permissions granted to them.

### 7.2 Explicit Authority Boundaries

The platform is intended to distinguish between:

- Information gathering.
- Analysis and recommendations.
- Proposed plans.
- Validated plans.
- Authorized operations.
- Completed operations.

These states must not be treated as interchangeable.

A generated plan is not automatically an approved plan. A validated plan is not automatically permission to execute it.

### 7.3 Deterministic Enforcement

Where consequential operations are involved, authority and policy requirements should be enforced through explicit software controls rather than relying solely on model instructions.

The architecture favors:

- Defined permission contracts.
- Structured inputs and outputs.
- Validation before execution.
- Explicit approval gates.
- Controlled execution interfaces.
- Recorded execution results.
- Fail-closed behavior when required conditions are not satisfied.

### 7.4 Transparency and Provenance

The system should make relevant model activity and operational decisions inspectable.

Users and authorized reviewers should be able to distinguish source information, model-generated interpretation, validation results, human decisions, and executed actions.

### 7.5 Privacy and Local Control

The platform prioritizes locally controlled processing to reduce unnecessary dependence on external model services and to support greater control over research data.

Local execution does not eliminate security, privacy, or data-management responsibilities. Those requirements remain part of the system's design and evaluation.

8. **Traceability.** Relevant information and operational events should retain their provenance.

The proposed public benefit is not simply access to more powerful AI. It is the development of infrastructure and methods that make AI systems more understandable, accountable, controllable, and useful to people.

The initial research program will focus on developing and evaluating the underlying platform. Future applications, including ClearVoice, may provide opportunities to evaluate its public benefit in specific contexts.

The platform is currently at the architecture and planning stage. Its intended benefits must therefore be treated as research objectives and prospective outcomes, not demonstrated impact.

## 2. Public-Interest Problem

AI capabilities are advancing rapidly, but the ability to coordinate them responsibly remains a significant technical and governance challenge.

Several concerns motivate this research.

### 2.1 Limited transparency

AI-assisted workflows can involve multiple models, tools, intermediate decisions, and automated steps.

Without adequate records, a person may see the final output without being able to determine:
- How the original request was interpreted.
- Which models or tools were used.
- What tasks were delegated.
- Which outputs influenced the result.
- Whether contradictory information was encountered.
- Where human approval or intervention occurred.

This creates accountability and verification challenges.

### 2.2 Fragmented capabilities

Specialized AI systems may each perform useful tasks, but coordinating them into a coherent workflow introduces additional complexity.

A system must determine which capabilities are appropriate, manage dependencies, evaluate outputs, handle failures, and communicate results in a form that people can inspect.

The absence of effective coordination can limit the usefulness of otherwise capable tools.

### 2.3 Weak or unclear human control

Human oversight is not meaningful merely because a person can view a result after an automated process has completed.

Meaningful control requires the ability to understand proposed actions, authorize or reject them, intervene during execution, and review what occurred.

The research will investigate how these controls can be incorporated into the architecture itself.

### 2.4 Concentration and dependency

Dependence on externally controlled AI services can create challenges involving data control, availability, cost, privacy, and the ability to inspect or modify system behavior.

Local-first infrastructure offers one possible approach to reducing certain dependencies and giving operators greater control over their computing environment.

Local operation does not automatically guarantee privacy, security, or accountability. Those properties must be designed and evaluated.

### 2.5 Unequal access and application-specific barriers

AI systems may fail to serve people whose needs differ from the assumptions built into general-purpose interfaces and models.

Speech recognition, communication support, accessibility, and specialized knowledge work are examples of areas where adaptable and human-correctable AI systems may provide meaningful benefits.

blAIne's architecture is intended to support specialized applications without requiring each application to independently solve every orchestration and governance problem.

## 3. Proposed Public Benefit

blAIne seeks to contribute to the public interest in five principal areas.

### 3.1 Human agency and meaningful oversight

The platform is designed around the principle that human authority must remain central.

Its proposed capabilities include:
- Explicit authorization before designated actions.
- Human approval, rejection, revision, and cancellation.
- Visible task plans and delegated activities.
- Escalation of unresolved conflicts.
- Records of authorization and intervention.

The intended benefit is to make human oversight an operational part of AI-assisted work rather than a superficial review step.

### 3.2 Transparency and accountability

The platform is intended to maintain records that make its operation inspectable.

These records may include:
- Request and task histories.
- Model and tool selection.
- Delegation and execution records.
- Intermediate and final outputs.
- Evaluation results.
- Authorization decisions.
- Errors, failures, and interventions.

The intended benefit is improved ability to understand, investigate, reproduce, and evaluate AI-assisted processes.

### 3.3 More effective use of specialized AI capabilities

The platform is intended to coordinate specialized models and tools according to their respective capabilities.

This could support workflows that require multiple forms of analysis or execution while allowing each component to remain independently identifiable and evaluable.

The intended benefit is more useful integration of AI capabilities without treating any individual model as universally reliable.

### 3.4 Local control and technological agency

The proposed architecture prioritizes local-first operation where practical.

Potential benefits include:
- Greater control over where data is processed.
- Reduced dependence on external service availability.
- Greater flexibility in selecting and adapting models.
- More direct control over system configuration.
- Opportunities to conduct research without sending all operational data to external services.

The project will evaluate the practical costs and limitations of local operation rather than assume that it is always superior.

### 3.5 Reusable public-interest infrastructure

A central objective is to develop reusable orchestration and governance capabilities that could support multiple applications.

If successful, the platform could reduce the need for each application developer to independently build task coordination, authorization, traceability, and evaluation infrastructure.

The potential public benefit extends beyond one product or application.

## 8. Risks and Potential Negative Impacts

A public-interest AI project must identify risks created by its own technology.

### 8.1 Automation bias

Users may place excessive trust in coordinated model outputs.

Mitigation:
- Display uncertainty and limitations.
- Preserve access to underlying evidence.
- Make human review and intervention available.
- Avoid presenting model agreement as proof of correctness.

### 8.2 Errors amplified through coordination

Multiple models may repeat or reinforce an incorrect assumption.

Mitigation:
- Preserve source and model provenance.
- Evaluate outputs independently where appropriate.
- Test contradictory and adversarial cases.
- Escalate unresolved conflicts.

### 8.3 Privacy and security risks

Local infrastructure can still be exposed to unauthorized access, insecure configurations, or inappropriate data retention.

Mitigation:
- Apply data minimization.
- Establish access controls.
- Document data handling and retention.
- Evaluate security and backup procedures.
- Obtain appropriate consent for personal data.

### 8.4 Excessive complexity

A multi-model architecture may introduce operational complexity, additional failure points, and greater resource consumption.

Mitigation:
- Measure overhead.
- Use modular interfaces.
- Document dependencies.
- Maintain understandable execution records.
- Compare orchestration against simpler alternatives.

### 8.5 Unequal access

A locally operated multi-workstation platform may be expensive and difficult for smaller organizations or individuals to reproduce.

Mitigation:
- Document hardware and operating requirements.
- Investigate opportunities for modular deployment.
- Evaluate lower-resource configurations.
- Prioritize reusable software and documented methods.

### 8.6 Misuse and unauthorized actions

An orchestration system may coordinate capabilities in ways that produce harmful or unintended outcomes.

Mitigation:
- Establish capability boundaries.
- Restrict designated actions through authorization controls.
- Record execution histories.
- Provide cancellation and intervention mechanisms.
- Evaluate misuse scenarios and failure conditions.
