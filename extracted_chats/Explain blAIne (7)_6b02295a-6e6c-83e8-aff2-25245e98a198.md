## 1. Purpose

The purpose of this system is to provide a structured, transparent, and accountable pathway through which human observations can reach the people, departments, organizations, or authorities best equipped to understand and address them.

People should not need to possess specialized knowledge, understand institutional structures, know the appropriate terminology, or correctly identify the significance of an observation before they can contribute it.

The system should help bridge that gap.

An individual may observe an event, experience a problem, identify an inconsistency, recognize a potential risk, or encounter something that warrants investigation. The system should provide a means of capturing that observation, preserving its context, evaluating its relevance, and directing it toward an appropriate destination.

The system exists to improve human capability and agency, not to replace human judgment or authority.

## 2. Foundational design philosophy

The architecture is governed by the following principles.

### 2.1 Transparency

Systems should operate transparently by default, subject to legitimate privacy, confidentiality, and security requirements.

People should be able to understand:
- What the system does.
- What happens to information they submit.
- How observations are processed.
- What criteria influence classification and routing.
- Who or what has handled an observation.
- What decisions have been made and why.
- What limitations or uncertainties remain.

Restrictions on visibility must have legitimate reasons and appropriate controls. Privacy and security protections should not become general justifications for unnecessary opacity.

### 2.2 Honesty

The system must accurately represent its knowledge, actions, limitations, and uncertainty.

It must distinguish:
- Observations from interpretations.
- Reports from verified findings.
- Evidence from assumptions.
- Automated suggestions from human decisions.
- Confirmed outcomes from unresolved matters.

The system must not manufacture certainty, conceal failures, misrepresent processing, or imply that an observation has been addressed when it has not.

### 2.3 Human authority

The system exists to support human understanding and action.

Automated processing may assist with organization, analysis, classification, recommendations, and routing. It must not silently acquire authority that belongs to people or institutions.

Human decision-making authority, responsibility, and accountability must remain identifiable.

### 2.4 Human agency

People should have meaningful opportunities to contribute observations, understand how their contributions are handled, challenge interpretations, correct information, and receive appropriate feedback.

The system should reduce the knowledge and procedural barriers that prevent people from participating in processes affecting them.

### 2.5 Evidence and provenance

Information must retain sufficient context and provenance to support responsible interpretation.

The system should preserve the distinction between what was originally submitted, what was subsequently added, what was inferred, and what was established through investigation.

### 2.6 Correctability

The system must be capable of receiving corrections and learning that previous interpretations, classifications, or routing decisions were incomplete or incorrect.

A system that cannot be corrected by relevant human observations risks becoming disconnected from reality.

## 3. Core operating concept

The system follows an observation-to-response lifecycle.

1. A person contributes an observation.
2. The system captures and preserves the original submission.
3. Relevant context is established.
4. The observation is evaluated and may be classified.
5. The system identifies an appropriate destination or requests human assistance.
6. An authorized person or department receives the observation.
7. The observation is reviewed and addressed through an appropriate process.
8. Outcomes, explanations, and relevant corrections are recorded.
9. Appropriate feedback is made available to the contributor, subject to privacy and security restrictions.

The process should not require the contributor to understand the internal organization or governance structure.

## 4. Functional architecture

### 4.1 Observation intake

The intake component provides the entry point for human observations.

Potential submission methods include:
- Structured forms.
- Free-text submissions.
- Supporting documents and evidence.
- Images or other media.
- Interviews or facilitated submissions.
- Referrals from other people or organizations.
- Future integrations with external reporting systems.

Intake should accommodate different levels of technical knowledge, literacy, accessibility, and familiarity with the subject.

The contributor should be able to describe what they observed without being forced to provide a definitive interpretation.

### 4.2 Observation preservation

The original submission should be preserved as a distinct record.

Subsequent processing must not silently overwrite the original observation.

The record should distinguish original information from later additions, annotations, classifications, translations, summaries, and investigative findings.

Where appropriate, the contributor should be able to supplement or correct their submission while preserving the history of changes.

### 4.3 Context and interpretation

The system should help establish the context necessary for understanding an observation.

This may involve:
- Identifying the subject or technology involved.
- Establishing relevant dates and locations.
- Identifying affected individuals or groups.
- Identifying supporting evidence.
- Recognizing potentially related observations.
- Identifying missing information.
- Recording uncertainty and conflicting accounts.

Automated assistance may help organize and interpret information, but its outputs must remain distinguishable from the original submission and verified findings.

The system must not treat a classification as proof.

### 4.4 Triage and classification

Observations may need to be categorized according to their subject, potential significance, urgency, and appropriate handling requirements.

Classification should help determine what expertise or authority is needed.

The system should support:
- Multiple relevant categories.
- Uncertainty and provisional classifications.
- Conflicting interpretations.
- Human review.
- Reclassification when new information becomes available.

The contributor should not bear responsibility for knowing the correct institutional classification.

### 4.5 Routing and referral

Routing is a central architectural function.

The system should identify the person, department, organization, or authority best positioned to handle an observation.

Routing decisions may consider:
- Subject-matter responsibility.
- Institutional jurisdiction.
- Relevant expertise.
- Geographic or legal jurisdiction.
- Urgency.
- Privacy and access restrictions.
- Existing case ownership.
- Potential conflicts of interest.

The system should support referral, escalation, reassignment, and acknowledgment of receipt.

If the appropriate destination cannot be established, the observation should enter a clearly identified unresolved state rather than disappear or be routed arbitrarily.

Routing recommendations and actual routing decisions must be distinguishable.

### 4.6 Human review and response

Authorized recipients should be able to review observations and determine appropriate next steps.

Possible actions include:
- Request additional information.
- Accept responsibility
