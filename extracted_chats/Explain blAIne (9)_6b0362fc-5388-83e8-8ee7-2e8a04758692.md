## 1. Purpose

The purpose of blAIne systems is to benefit human beings by improving their ability to understand, participate in, make decisions about, and act within the systems that affect their lives.



Technology should expand human capability and agency. It should not displace human authority, conceal information, create unnecessary dependencies, or become an unquestionable authority in its own right.



This is not a monetization objective. The fundamental purpose is human benefit.



All system capabilities, processes, and design decisions must remain consistent with that purpose.

## 2. The Three Foundational Principles

### 2.1 Transparency

Systems should be transparent and open to examination by default.



People should be able to ask questions about a system and receive meaningful, understandable explanations of:

- Its purpose and intended functions.
- How it operates.
- How information is collected, processed, stored, and used.
- How decisions, recommendations, and outputs are produced.
- What its capabilities and limitations are.
- What assumptions and dependencies affect its operation.
- What known flaws, failures, and unresolved problems exist.
- How the system is governed, maintained, and changed.

Transparency must be substantive rather than performative. Publishing information that people cannot reasonably understand does not necessarily make a system transparent.



Legitimate privacy, confidentiality, and security requirements may restrict access to particular information. Such restrictions should be proportionate, justified, and appropriately documented. They must not become general excuses for unnecessary opacity.

### 2.2 Honesty

Systems must honestly represent themselves, their information, their capabilities, and their outputs.



A system must not claim certainty where uncertainty exists, capability where limitations exist, or success where failure has occurred.



Known flaws, limitations, failures, and inconsistencies must be identified and exposed so they can be understood and corrected.



This includes:

- Acknowledging uncertainty.
- Distinguishing evidence from inference.
- Identifying incomplete or unreliable information.
- Flagging known defects and operational problems.
- Recording failures and unsuccessful operations.
- Correcting inaccurate information.
- Preserving relevant histories of changes and corrections.
- Communicating when the system cannot reliably perform a requested function.

Concealing deficiencies to preserve the appearance of reliability is incompatible with this principle.



A system's credibility should come from its demonstrated honesty, not from an assertion that it is flawless.

### 2.3 Agency

Human agency is the ultimate objective of the system.



Every capability must be evaluated according to whether it benefits people and strengthens their ability to understand, decide, participate, and act.



Systems should:

- Make useful information accessible.
- Reduce unnecessary barriers to participation.
- Help people understand complex processes.
- Preserve meaningful opportunities for human judgment.
- Allow people to question and challenge outputs.
- Support informed decisions.
- Make relevant processes and responsibilities understandable.
- Avoid unnecessary dependence on automated systems.
- Preserve human authority over decisions affecting people.

Automation and artificial intelligence may expand human capability, but their existence does not establish their authority.



The system must remain a tool serving human beings.

## 3. The Relationship Between the Principles

Transparency, Honesty, and Agency are interdependent.



Transparency without honesty can expose information that is misleading.



Honesty without transparency can leave people unable to examine or understand what a system is doing.



Neither provides meaningful agency if people cannot use what they learn to question, decide, participate, or act.



Together, these principles establish the conditions under which technology can meaningfully support human agency.

## 4. Universal Application

These principles apply to the entire system lifecycle.



They govern:

- Initial conception and design.
- Architecture and implementation.
- Data collection and processing.
- Artificial intelligence and automation.
- Information presentation and communication.
- Governance and authorization.
- Maintenance and operation.
- Evaluation and validation.
- Modification and correction.
- Retirement or replacement.

They apply to the system itself as well as to the processes and information it manages.



A system must not demand transparency, honesty, or accountability from its participants while exempting its own operation from those expectations.

## 5. Non-Concealment and Correctability

A system must be capable of acknowledging that it is wrong, incomplete, unreliable, or no longer appropriate for its intended purpose.



Problems should be visible to the people responsible for addressing them, and relevant limitations should be visible to people relying on the system.



The ability to identify and correct deficiencies is an essential characteristic of a trustworthy system.



The objective is not to build systems that claim to be perfect.



The objective is to build systems that remain understandable, honest about their imperfections, and capable of improvement.

## 6. Human Benefit Over Institutional or Commercial Objectives

System success must ultimately be evaluated by its contribution to human benefit and agency.



Commercial success, organizational convenience, automation rates, efficiency, and technical sophistication are not substitutes for this objective.



They may be useful considerations, but they cannot independently justify design decisions that undermine Transparency, Honesty, or Agency.



The system must not treat people merely as sources of data, resources to optimize, or obstacles to automation.



People are the reason the system exists.

## 7. Foundational Design Test

Every significant design decision should be examined through three questions:

1. **Transparency:** Can people understand what the system is doing, why it is doing it, and what its limitations are?
2. **Honesty:** Does the system accurately represent its information, capabilities, uncertainty, failures, and results?
3. **Agency:** Does this decision improve or preserve people's ability to understand, decide, participate, and act?

A decision that conflicts with these principles must be reconsidered, justified against legitimate constraints, or rejected.

## 8. Governing Statement

Transparency makes systems understandable.



Honesty makes their representations trustworthy.



Agency makes their capabilities meaningful to human beings.



**Everything exists to serve human benefit.**



These are not optional features or aspirational additions. They are the foundational principles governing the conception, design, implementation, and operation of blAIne systems.

## Purpose

This repository contains the foundational principles, governance framework, architectural requirements, and system design artifacts for human-centered systems developed under the blAIne initiative.

All architecture and design decisions must remain consistent with the following principles:

- **Transparency:** Systems must be understandable and open to meaningful examination.
- **Honesty:** Systems must accurately represent their information, capabilities, limitations, uncertainty, and failures.
- **Agency:** Systems must serve human beings and strengthen their ability to understand, decide, participate, and act.

These principles govern the entire system lifecycle, including conception, design, implementation, operation, evaluation, modification, and retirement.

This repository establishes reusable foundations for human-agency-centered systems.

Its scope includes:

- Foundational design principles.
- Governance and accountability.
- Human authority and authorization.

1. Preserve human authority over consequential decisions.

4. Maintain traceability and meaningful histories of changes.
5. Expose uncertainty, deficiencies, and operational failures.
6. Support human review, correction, challenge, and appeal.
7. Avoid unnecessary dependence on automated decision-making.

**Document type:** Governance Framework

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the governance framework for the design, development, evaluation, modification, and maintenance of systems within the Human Agency Systems Architecture.

Its purpose is to ensure that architectural decisions remain consistent with the foundational principles of Transparency, Honesty, and Agency.

Governance must preserve human authority, establish accountability, make decisions understandable, and provide a reliable means of identifying and correcting problems.

## 2. Governing Authority

The blAIne Foundational Design Charter is the highest-level governing document for this architecture.

All subordinate governance documents, architectural specifications, requirements, and implementations must remain consistent with it.

No technical implementation, organizational preference, efficiency objective, or automated recommendation overrides the foundational principles.

Where a conflict exists, the conflict must be documented and resolved rather than silently incorporated into the architecture.

## 3. Human Design Authority

Human beings retain authority over the purpose, direction, governance, and consequential decisions of systems developed under this framework.

For this architecture:

- Blaine Smith is the originating designer and authority for the foundational concepts and architectural direction established in this repository.
- Design decisions must be explicitly attributable to the person or authority responsible for making them.
- Technical recommendations do not constitute authorization.
- AI-generated content, analysis, or implementation proposals do not independently establish design authority.
- Decisions affecting people must have identifiable human responsibility.

Where an architecture is adopted by another organization, its governance must explicitly identify that organization's legitimate decision-makers and responsibilities. Repository authorship does not automatically confer authority over another organization's operations, participants, or information.

## 4. Architectural Decision Process

Significant architectural decisions should follow a documented process.

### 4.1 Identify the Decision

Record:

- The issue or requirement being addressed.
- The reason a decision is necessary.
- The affected systems, components, and people.
- Relevant constraints and dependencies.

### 4.2 Examine Alternatives

Identify reasonable alternatives and their implications.

Evaluation should consider:

- Human benefit and agency.
- Transparency and explainability.
- Information integrity.
- Security and privacy.
- Operational feasibility.
- Reliability and maintainability.
- Accountability and reversibility.

### 4.3 Evaluate Against Foundational Principles

Every significant decision must be evaluated against Transparency, Honesty, and Agency.

Any conflict, limitation, or trade-off must be explicitly documented.

### 4.4 Record the Decision

The decision record should identify:

- The decision.
- Its rationale.
- Alternatives considered.
- Known limitations and risks.
- The responsible decision-maker.
- Supporting evidence.
- Date and version.
- Conditions that would justify reconsideration.

### 4.5 Validate the Result

Implementation must be evaluated against the decision's stated requirements and acceptance criteria.

A decision is not considered successfully implemented merely because code has been produced or a component appears operational.

## 5. AI-Assisted Development

AI tools may assist with:

- Research and technical evaluation.
- Organizing and formalizing design concepts.
- Identifying possible architectural approaches.
- Documentation.
- Code and test generation.
- Troubleshooting.
- Reviewing implementations and identifying potential defects.

AI tools do not possess independent authority to:

- Establish system purpose.
- Change foundational principles.
- Approve architectural decisions.
- Override human authorization.
- Conceal implementation limitations.
- Represent unverified results as confirmed outcomes.

AI-generated recommendations must remain distinguishable from human decisions.

Material AI contributions should be documented where their provenance is relevant to understanding the design or implementation history.

## 6. Change Control

Changes to foundational or architectural artifacts must be traceable.

A proposed change should identify:

- The artifact being changed.
- The reason for the change.
- The existing behavior or requirement.
- The proposed modification.
- Expected benefits.
- Potential consequences and risks.
- Effects on dependent components.
- The responsible decision-maker.

Changes to approved foundational principles require explicit approval from the originating designer.

Changes to subordinate architecture must be reviewed for consistency with the governing charter.

Previously approved artifacts should not be silently overwritten. Their revision history should remain available.

## 7. Disagreement and Challenge

Architectural decisions must be open to reasoned examination.

A person identifying a potential problem should have a means to:

- Question the decision.
- Present contrary evidence.
- Identify overlooked consequences.
- Request clarification.
- Propose an alternative.
- Request reconsideration.

Disagreement must not be treated as evidence of noncompliance or as a reason to suppress valid concerns.

Challenges should be evaluated on their substance and supporting evidence.

## 8. Defects, Failures, and Corrections

Known defects and failures must be recorded and communicated to the people responsible for addressing them.

The governance process should distinguish:

- Confirmed defects.
- Suspected defects.
- Operational incidents.
- Design limitations.
- Unresolved questions.
- Corrective actions.
- Verified resolutions.

A correction must not erase the history of the original problem or create a misleading impression that the problem never existed.

## 9. Documentation and Traceability

Governance records should provide a meaningful history of:

- Architectural decisions.
- Approvals and authorization.
- Changes and revisions.
- Challenges and their disposition.
- Known limitations and risks.
- Validation results.
- Defects and corrective actions.

Documentation should be understandable and sufficiently detailed to support independent examination.

Where privacy, security, or confidentiality requires restricted access, the restriction and its justification should be documented.

## 10. Periodic Review

The architecture should be reviewed when:

- Foundational requirements change.
- Significant new capabilities are introduced.
- New risks or limitations are identified.
- Operational evidence challenges existing assumptions.
- A system's purpose or affected population changes.
- A significant incident exposes a governance weakness.

Review must examine whether the architecture continues to serve human benefit and comply with its foundational principles.

## 11. Governing Statement

Architectural authority must remain identifiable.

Decisions must remain explainable.

Changes must remain traceable.

Problems must remain visible and correctable.

AI may assist the work, but human beings retain responsibility and authority.

<WritingBlock id="49217" variant="document"># System Architecture Principles

**Document type:** Architectural Principles  
**Version:** 1.0 — Initial Draft  
**Status:** Draft  
**Governing authority:** blAIne Foundational Design Charter  
**Origin:** Blaine Smith

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the architectural principles that guide the structure, behavior, and interaction of systems developed under the Human Agency Systems Architecture.

The Foundational Design Charter establishes why these systems exist and the principles governing their development.

This document establishes how those principles must influence architectural decisions.

These principles apply across individual systems, interconnected systems, shared services, information flows, and future extensions.

## 2. Human Authority

Systems must preserve identifiable human authority over their purpose, governance, and consequential decisions.

Architectural design must:

- Identify the human authorities responsible for system governance.
- Distinguish recommendations from authorized decisions.
- Make decision ownership identifiable.
- Preserve meaningful human review and intervention.
- Prevent automated components from silently acquiring authority.
- Provide mechanisms for challenging and correcting consequential outputs.

Automation may perform authorized operations, but its authority must derive from explicit human-defined governance.

## 3. Separation of Information and Interpretation

Systems must preserve the distinction between information as originally received and interpretations subsequently applied to it.

Architectural components should distinguish:

- Original submissions and observations.
- Context supplied by the contributor.
- Supporting materials.
- Annotations and additions.
- Classification and categorization.
- Translations and summaries.
- Automated analysis and inference.
- Human assessments.
- Verified findings.
- Decisions and resulting actions.

These categories must not be silently conflated.

An interpretation must not replace the original information from which it was derived.

Where information is incomplete, disputed, uncertain, or unverified, that condition must remain identifiable.

## 4. Provenance and Traceability

Information and consequential system actions should retain sufficient provenance to establish their origin and history.

The architecture should support:

- Identification of original sources.
- Attribution of additions and modifications.
- Timestamps and relevant context.
- Identification of automated processing.
- Identification of human review and authorization.
- Version and correction histories.
- Traceability between source information and derived outputs.

Provenance must support examination without unnecessarily exposing protected personal information.

## 5. Modularity and Separation of Responsibilities

Systems should be organized into components with identifiable responsibilities.

Components should have clear boundaries, defined interfaces, and documented dependencies.

Architectural separation should make it possible to:

- Examine individual functions.
- Replace or improve components.
- Isolate failures.
- Validate behavior.
- Control access.
- Maintain accountability.
- Reuse appropriate capabilities without creating unnecessary coupling.

Modularity must serve understandability and maintainability rather than become an excuse for excessive complexity.

## 6. Explicit Authority and Permission Boundaries

Access to information and authority to act are separate concerns.

The architecture must distinguish:

- Who can access information.
- Who can contribute information.
- Who can interpret or classify information.
- Who can review or validate information.
- Who can make decisions.
- Who can authorize actions.
- Who can modify system configuration.
- Who can examine governance and operational records.

Permissions should reflect legitimate responsibilities and be limited to what those responsibilities require.

Technical access must not automatically confer decision-making authority.

## 7. Transparency and Observability

System operation must be sufficiently observable to support meaningful examination.

The architecture should make it possible to understand:

- What a component is responsible for.
- What information it receives.
- What processing it performs.
- What information it produces.
- What dependencies it relies upon.
- What decisions or recommendations it generates.
- What limitations affect its operation.
- What failures or exceptions have occurred.

Observability should support both operational troubleshooting and broader accountability.

Internal technical visibility alone is insufficient if the information cannot be translated into explanations meaningful to the people affected.

## 8. Failure Visibility and Correctability

Systems must be designed to expose and manage failures rather than conceal them.

Architectural components should support:

- Detection of unsuccessful operations.
- Identification of incomplete processing.
- Reporting of degraded functionality.
- Preservation of relevant error information.
- Identification of affected information and processes.
- Corrective action and reassessment.
- Verification that corrections were successful.

A failure must not be represented as a successful outcome.

Where the system cannot reliably complete an operation, that limitation must be communicated.

## 9. Human Participation and Challenge

Systems should provide meaningful ways for people to participate in processes that affect them.

Depending on the system's purpose and responsibilities, this may include:

- Submitting information.
- Providing context.
- Correcting inaccurate information.
- Challenging interpretations.
- Requesting review.
- Seeking clarification.
- Escalating unresolved matters.
- Receiving appropriate feedback.

Participation must not require people to possess specialized technical knowledge that the system is intended to make unnecessary.

## 10. Privacy and Security

Privacy and security must be addressed as architectural requirements.

Systems should:

- Collect only information necessary for legitimate purposes.
- Establish clear access boundaries.
- Protect sensitive information.
- Document information handling and retention requirements.
- Prevent unauthorized modification or disclosure.
- Preserve accountability for access and consequential actions.
- Explain restrictions where disclosure is appropriate.

Privacy and security controls must be proportionate to the risks and must not become general justifications for unnecessary opacity.

## 11. Interoperability and Adaptability

Systems should be capable of interacting with other systems where doing so serves legitimate human needs.

Interfaces and dependencies should be documented.

Interoperability must not require surrendering control of information, authority, or governance to an external system.

Architecture should accommodate changing requirements and technologies without unnecessarily compromising established information, provenance, or accountability.

## 12. Reversibility and Maintainability

Significant architectural decisions should consider whether changes can be made safely and whether systems can be maintained over time.

Where practical, systems should support:

- Replacing individual components.
- Reverting unsuccessful changes.
- Recovering from failures.
- Migrating information.
- Preserving historical records.
- Retiring components without destroying unrelated capabilities.

The architecture should avoid unnecessary dependencies that make systems difficult to repair or replace.

## 13. Evidence-Based Validation

Architectural claims must be distinguishable from demonstrated system behavior.

Validation should identify:

- What was intended.
- What was implemented.
- What was tested.
- What results were observed.
- What remains untested.
- What limitations remain unresolved.

A documented requirement is not proof of implementation.

An implementation is not proof of correct operation.

A successful test is evidence only within the scope and conditions of that test.

## 14. Application of These Principles

These principles apply to:

- Individual system components.
- Data and information models.
- Automated processing.
- Artificial intelligence integrations.
- Human interfaces.
- Governance mechanisms.
- External integrations.
- Operational processes.
- Evaluation and maintenance.

Significant architectural decisions should be recorded through the Architectural Decision Record process.

Conflicts with these principles must be documented and resolved through the established governance process.

## 15. Governing Statement

Architecture must make human authority identifiable, information trustworthy, system behavior understandable, and correction possible.

Technical capability is valuable only when it serves human benefit.

**Document type:** Requirements Governance Standard

**Governing authority:** blAIne Foundational Design Charter

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes a consistent method for defining, organizing, documenting, validating, and maintaining requirements within the Human Agency Systems Architecture.

Requirements translate system purpose, governing principles, and architectural decisions into specific conditions that a system must satisfy.

The objective is to ensure that requirements remain understandable, attributable, testable, and traceable throughout the system lifecycle.

## 2. Requirement Authority

Requirements originate from identifiable human-defined needs, objectives, constraints, and design decisions.

Requirements may be informed by:

- Foundational design principles.
- System purpose and intended capabilities.
- Human needs and operational objectives.
- Architectural decisions.
- Technical constraints.
- Security and privacy considerations.
- Operational evidence.
- Validation findings.
- Identified deficiencies and risks.

AI-assisted analysis may help identify, organize, refine, or evaluate requirements. It does not independently establish their authority or approval.

Every approved requirement must have an identifiable source and responsible human authority.

## 3. Requirement Classification

Requirements should be classified according to their function.

### 3.1 Foundational Requirements

Requirements derived directly from the foundational principles and governing documents.

They establish conditions that subordinate architecture and implementations must respect.

### 3.2 Functional Requirements

Requirements defining what a system or component must do.

They describe capabilities, processes, interactions, and expected behavior.

### 3.3 Non-Functional Requirements

Requirements defining how a system must operate.

Examples include:

- Reliability.
- Performance.
- Availability.
- Maintainability.
- Scalability.
- Usability.
- Accessibility.
- Interoperability.

### 3.4 Governance Requirements

Requirements establishing authority, responsibility, authorization, oversight, and accountability.

### 3.5 Information Requirements

Requirements governing information structure, integrity, provenance, classification, retention, access, and processing.

### 3.6 Security and Privacy Requirements

Requirements governing protection against unauthorized access, disclosure, modification, misuse, and loss.

### 3.7 Operational Requirements

Requirements governing deployment, configuration, monitoring, maintenance, recovery, and retirement.

### 3.8 Validation Requirements

Requirements establishing how system behavior, capabilities, and compliance must be demonstrated.

## 4. Requirement Identification

Every formal requirement must have a unique identifier.

Identifiers should remain stable even when wording, implementation, or status changes.

Recommended prefixes:

| Prefix | Requirement category |
|---|---|
| FND | Foundational |
| FUN | Functional |
| NFR | Non-functional |
| GOV | Governance |
| INF | Information |
| SEC | Security and privacy |
| OPS | Operational |
| VAL | Validation |

Example:

`GOV-001`

The identifier should not encode assumptions about implementation or imply a priority that may change.

## 5. Requirement Structure

Each formal requirement should contain:

**Identifier:** Unique requirement ID.

**Title:** Short descriptive name.

**Category:** Requirement classification.

**Statement:** A clear description of what the system must satisfy.

**Rationale:** Why the requirement exists.

**Source:** The originating principle, need, decision, evidence, or constraint.

**Priority:** Its relative importance.

**Status:** Proposed, Approved, Implemented, Validated, Rejected, Superseded, or Deferred.

**Responsible Authority:** The human authority responsible for approving the requirement.

**Dependencies:** Related requirements or external conditions.

**Acceptance Criteria:** Observable conditions used to determine whether the requirement is satisfied.

**Validation Evidence:** References to tests, reviews, demonstrations, or other supporting evidence.

**History:** Relevant revisions and status changes.

## 6. Requirement Quality

Requirements should be:

- Clear and understandable.
- Specific enough to guide implementation.
- Consistent with governing principles.
- Traceable to an identifiable source.
- Verifiable through defined acceptance criteria.
- Free of unnecessary implementation assumptions.
- Explicit about relevant constraints and dependencies.

Ambiguous, contradictory, incomplete, or untestable requirements must be identified rather than silently treated as settled.

Where a requirement cannot yet be fully defined, its unresolved elements should be documented.

## 7. Traceability

Requirements must maintain meaningful relationships with the artifacts that establish, implement, and validate them.

Traceability should connect:

1. Foundational principles.
2. System objectives.
3. Requirements.
4. Architectural decisions.
5. Architectural components.
6. Implementations.
7. Tests and validation evidence.
8. Identified defects and corrective actions.

A requirement should be traceable forward to its implementation and validation where applicable.

It should also be traceable backward to the purpose, principle, or need that justifies its existence.

Traceability must expose:

- Requirements without an identified source.
- Requirements without an architectural allocation.
- Implementations without an identified requirement or approved decision.
- Requirements without validation evidence.
- Unresolved dependencies.
- Conflicting or duplicated requirements.

## 8. Requirement Changes

Requirements may change as understanding improves, evidence develops, or system conditions evolve.

Changes must preserve a meaningful history.

A requirement change should document:

- The original requirement.
- The proposed modification.
- The reason for the change.
- Supporting evidence.
- Affected requirements and components.
- Consequences for existing implementations.
- Responsible approval authority.
- Updated acceptance criteria.

Approved requirements must not be silently rewritten.

Superseded requirements should remain historically accessible.

## 9. Validation and Verification

Requirement status must accurately reflect demonstrated progress.

The following distinctions apply:

- **Proposed:** Defined but not approved.
- **Approved:** Authorized for implementation.
- **Implemented:** Incorporated into a system or component.
- **Validated:** Evidence demonstrates that the acceptance criteria have been satisfied.
- **Rejected:** Explicitly declined by the responsible authority.
- **Superseded:** Replaced by a subsequent requirement.
- **Deferred:** Intentionally postponed.

Implementation alone does not establish validation.

Validation evidence must identify the conditions under which the requirement was evaluated and any limitations affecting the result.

## 10. Foundational Principle Evaluation

Requirements must remain consistent with Transparency, Honesty, and Agency.

**Transparency:** Can the requirement and its purpose be understood and examined?

**Honesty:** Does the requirement encourage accurate representation of capabilities, limitations, evidence, and results?

**Agency:** Does the requirement serve human benefit and preserve or strengthen meaningful human participation and authority?

Requirements that conflict with these principles must be reconsidered through the established governance process.

## 11. Requirements Repository

Formal requirements should be maintained in a structured and version-controlled form.

The repository should support:

- Unique requirement identifiers.
- Consistent classification.
- Revision history.
- Relationship tracking.
- Approval status.
- Validation references.
- Identification of unresolved matters.

The chosen storage format may evolve as the architecture develops, provided that requirements remain accessible, understandable, and traceable.

## 12. Governing Statement

Requirements must have identifiable origins, clear meaning, responsible human authority, and demonstrable outcomes.

What a system is intended to do must remain distinguishable from what it actually does.

What has been implemented must remain distinguishable from what has been validated.

**Transparency • Honesty • Agency**</WritingBlock>

- Human participants.

- Component purpose.

- Relevant interfaces.

- People and organizations.

- Responsible parties.

- What the system may perform automatically.
- What requires human authorization.

- What actions are prohibited.
- Who is responsible for consequential outcomes.

- Information sources.

- What capability it provides.

Relevant distinctions include:

- Observation.
- Statement.
- Claim.
- Evidence.
- Context.
- Interpretation.
- Classification.
- Inference.
- Analysis.
- Assessment.
- Finding.
- Decision.

These terms must not be treated as interchangeable.

For example, an observation does not automatically constitute verified evidence, and an automated classification does not establish the truth of the underlying information.

Interpretations should identify their sources, assumptions, and relevant uncertainty.

## 6. Information Quality and Integrity

Information quality should be evaluated according to its intended use.

Relevant characteristics include:

- Accuracy.
- Completeness.
- Consistency.
- Timeliness.
- Relevance.
- Reliability.
- Provenance.
- Context.
- Verifiability.

A system should identify known quality limitations.

Incomplete, contradictory, outdated, or unreliable information must not be silently presented as complete or authoritative.

Information quality assessments should remain distinguishable from the original information itself.

## 7. Processing and Transformation

Information processing must be attributable and understandable.

Where information is transformed, the system should document:

- The source information.
- The transformation performed.
- The purpose.
- The method or process used.
- Relevant assumptions.
- The responsible component or actor.
- The resulting information.
- Known limitations or potential information loss.

Examples include:

- Translation.
- Summarization.
- Classification.
- Extraction.
- Normalization.
- Aggregation.
- Statistical analysis.
- AI-generated interpretation.

Derived information must remain traceable to its sources.

Transformations must not create the false impression that generated or summarized content is an original source record.

AI systems may assist with organizing, analyzing, classifying, summarizing, translating, or interpreting information.

AI-generated outputs must remain identifiable as generated or AI-assisted outputs where that distinction is material.

The architecture should preserve:

- Relevant source information.
- Model or service identification where available.
- Processing context.
- Instructions or parameters where material.
- Output history.
- Human review or modification.
- Known uncertainty and limitations.

AI-generated interpretations must not automatically become verified facts, authoritative classifications, or approved decisions.

Human review requirements should be proportionate to the consequences of relying on the output.

Information access must be governed by legitimate purpose and authorization.

The architecture should distinguish:

- Access to view information.
- Access to contribute information.
- Access to modify information.
- Access to classify or interpret information.
- Access to share or disclose information.
- Authority to approve consequential uses.

Where applicable, information ownership, consent, confidentiality, and other rights must be respected.

People should receive understandable explanations of relevant information practices and restrictions.

## 10. Privacy and Data Minimization

Systems should collect and retain only information necessary for legitimate purposes.

Information handling should consider:

- Sensitivity.
- Identifiability.
- Collection necessity.
- Intended use.
- Consent or other applicable authority.
- Access restrictions.
- Retention requirements.
- Sharing and disclosure risks.
- Potential harm from misuse.

Privacy protections should be designed into the architecture rather than added only after implementation.

## 11. Information Security

Information must be protected against unauthorized access, disclosure, alteration, destruction, and misuse.

Controls should be proportionate to the sensitivity and potential consequences of exposure.

Security measures should include appropriate combinations of:

- Authentication.
- Authorization.
- Access control.
- Encryption.
- Integrity protection.
- Backup and recovery.
- Audit records.
- Secure configuration.
- Incident response.

Security controls and their limitations should be documented.

## 12. Correction and Dispute

Systems must support the identification and correction of inaccurate information.

Where appropriate, people should be able to:

- Request correction.
- Provide additional context.
- Challenge an interpretation.
- Dispute a classification.
- Identify missing or misleading information.
- Request review.

Corrections should preserve relevant history.

Where information remains disputed, the system should represent that dispute rather than silently selecting one interpretation as established truth.

## 13. Retention and Disposition

Information retention must have an identifiable purpose and applicable authority.

Systems should define:

- Retention periods or criteria.
- Archival requirements.
- Deletion procedures.
- Legal or operational preservation obligations.
- Backup handling.
- Disposition responsibilities.

Information should not be retained indefinitely without justification.

Nor should information be destroyed in a manner that improperly eliminates legitimate rights, accountability, or necessary historical records.

## 14. Information Sharing and Interoperability

Information sharing must have a defined purpose and appropriate authorization.

When information moves between systems, relevant context, provenance, classification, and limitations should accompany it.

The receiving system must not assume that incoming information is accurate, complete, or authoritative merely because it originated from another system.

Interfaces should make relevant transformations and losses of information identifiable.

## 15. Information Incidents and Failures

Information-related incidents must be documented and addressed.

Examples include:

- Unauthorized access.
- Accidental disclosure.
- Data corruption.
- Loss of provenance.
- Incorrect transformations.
- Misclassification.
- Unintended disclosure.
- Improper deletion.
- Inaccurate or misleading outputs.

Incident records should identify known facts, uncertainty, affected information, corrective actions, and relevant notifications.

Unconfirmed suspicions must remain distinguishable from verified incidents.

## 16. Auditability

Information handling must be sufficiently traceable to support legitimate examination.

Relevant records may include:

- Access events.
- Processing events.
- Modifications.
- Sharing and disclosure.
- Authorization.
- Corrections.
- Deletions.
- Administrative actions.
- System-generated transformations.

Audit mechanisms must themselves respect privacy and security requirements.

## 17. Foundational Principle Evaluation

**Transparency:** Can people understand how information is collected, processed, represented, stored, and shared?

**Honesty:** Does the system preserve distinctions between original information, interpretation, uncertainty, and verified findings?

**Agency:** Can people understand and exercise their legitimate rights and participate meaningfully in processes involving their information?

Information governance must serve human benefit rather than treating information collection or accumulation as an independent objective.

## 18. Governing Statement

Information must retain its context, origin, and history.

Interpretation must remain distinguishable from observation.

Generated outputs must remain distinguishable from original sources.

Corrections must remain traceable.

Information exists to serve legitimate human purposes, not to become an authority over the people it represents.

**Transparency • Honesty • Agency**</WritingBlock>
